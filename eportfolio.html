Algebra Revision Worksheet

Know Students and How They Learn — Stage 5 Mathematics

An algebra revision worksheet for a mixed-ability secondary Mathematics class. Covers equations with variables on both sides, brackets, algebraic fractions, changing the subject, and word problems — with plain English instructions, structured working lines, and colour-coded sections.

Learning Goal

My goal is to design resources that meet students where they are. This worksheet deliberately lowers the entry barrier for students who struggle with dense textbook language while maintaining full Stage 5 rigour.

Why it addresses Standard 1

APST Standard 1 requires teachers to know how students learn and design accordingly. Instructions are in plain English to remove language barriers. Working lines reduce cognitive load. Questions are sequenced simple-to-complex following Vygotsky's zone of proximal development (Sullivan et al., 2020). Hattie (2009) identifies scaffolding as one of the highest-effect instructional strategies — it is built into every section here.

NESA Alignment

MA5.1-5NA, MA5.2-6NA — Algebraic expressions and linear equations, Stage 5.

Learning Reflection

"Accessibility is not about lowering standards — it is about removing unnecessary barriers so students can demonstrate what they actually know."

At a Glance

  • Stage 5, mixed-ability
  • Plain English instructions
  • Structured working lines
  • Colour-coded headers
  • 6 algebra topic areas

⚖ Ethics

Scaffolded materials distributed discreetly. No student publicly identified as needing support. Simplification must not reduce rigour — all tasks align to NESA outcomes.

References

Sullivan et al. (2020). Mathematics Education Research Journal, 32, 405–424.

Hattie, J. (2009). Visible Learning. Routledge.

A purpose-built PowerPoint lesson with learning intention, success criteria, worked examples, and practice questions — combined with a hands-on activity that gives abstract formulas a concrete physical meaning before symbolic practice begins.

Learning Goal

My goal is to develop deep pedagogical content knowledge — not just knowing the mathematics, but knowing how to represent it in multiple ways so all learners can access it.

Why it addresses Standard 2

APST Standard 2 requires teachers to know their content and how to teach it effectively. The PowerPoint sequences prerequisite concepts before complex applications. Anthony & Walshaw (2009) identify multi-representational teaching — visual, symbolic, and verbal — as the hallmark of effective mathematics instruction. The hands-on activity applies Bruner's (1966) enactive-iconic-symbolic model: students engage concretely before encountering the abstract formula, giving it meaning they can retrieve under assessment conditions.

NESA Alignment

MA5.2-1WM, MA5.2-2WM, MA5.2-3WM — Working mathematically, Stage 5. Applicable to any content strand benefiting from concrete exploration before symbolic application.

Learning Reflection

"The real professional skill is selecting which representation — hands-on, visual, or symbolic — will make content accessible to a specific group of students at a specific moment."

At a Glance

  • Stage 5, any topic
  • Purpose-built PowerPoint
  • Learning intention + success criteria
  • Hands-on concrete activity
  • Board work — live modelling

⚖ Ethics

Original diagrams and examples only. Hands-on materials must be low-cost and accessible. Digital resources also available in print for students without devices.

References

Anthony, G., & Walshaw, M. (2009). Journal of Mathematics Education, 2(2), 147–164.

Bruner, J. S. (1966). Toward a Theory of Instruction. Harvard University Press.

APST Standard 3

Financial Mathematics Worksheet

Plan for and Implement Effective Teaching and Learning — Stage 5 Mathematics

Question Sequence — Bloom's Taxonomy Applied (Kilpatrick et al., 2001)

Q1–3 · Recall & ProcedureBuild fluency
Q4–7 · ApplicationMixed scenarios
Q8 · AnalysisReverse reasoning
Q9–10 · SynthesisMulti-concept problems

A Financial Mathematics worksheet on income and overtime pay. Includes a reference table, two worked examples, and ten practice questions sequenced from basic procedure through reverse reasoning to multi-step synthesis.

Learning Goal

My goal is to plan sequences, not just tasks — designing questions that build meaningfully so students move from recall to genuine mathematical thinking without the difficulty increase feeling sudden.

Why it addresses Standard 3

APST Standard 3 requires deliberate, evidence-based planning. The ten questions reflect Bloom's Taxonomy in applied form (Kilpatrick et al., 2001). The lesson opens with a real-life hook connecting the mathematics to students' future working lives, consistent with Standard 3.4's requirement to select strategies appropriate to student context. Kilpatrick et al. (2001) argue procedural fluency and conceptual understanding must develop together — this sequencing ensures both.

NESA Alignment

MA5.1-1WM — Selects and applies appropriate strategies. Financial Mathematics content strand, Stage 5.

Learning Reflection

"The order of questions is itself a teaching decision. Moving the reverse problem to a later position — after fluency is established — changes what students are able to do with it."

At a Glance

  • Stage 5, Financial Mathematics
  • Time-and-a-half and double time
  • Reference table + 2 worked examples
  • 10 questions, progressive complexity
  • Real-world context throughout

⚖ Ethics

Names in examples must be fictional and culturally diverse. Real-world context motivates without exposing any student's personal financial circumstances.

References

Kilpatrick, J., Swafford, J., & Findell, B. (2001). Adding It Up. National Academy Press.

Anthony, G., & Walshaw, M. (2009). Journal of Mathematics Education, 2(2).

A strategic seating plan where every placement is a deliberate decision based on student learning needs — not alphabetical order or student preference. Students needing more support are positioned on the teacher's primary movement path, peer support pairs are intentionally selected, and disruptive social dynamics are separated without punitive framing.

Learning Goal

My goal is to treat classroom space as a teaching tool — thinking carefully about where each student sits and why, and being prepared to revise when observation shows it is not working.

Why it addresses Standard 4

APST Standard 4 requires teachers to create and maintain safe, inclusive environments. Wannarka & Ruhl (2008) found planned seating produces significantly higher on-task behaviour than self-selected arrangements. Marzano et al. (2003) identify proactive management as far more effective than reactive — the seating plan is the primary proactive tool available to every teacher before the first lesson begins.

APST Sub-Standards

4.1 — Support participation. 4.2 — Manage classroom activities. 4.3 — Manage challenging behaviour proactively.

Learning Reflection

"A seating plan is an architectural decision about how learning flows in the room. I will approach it analytically before meeting every new class."

At a Glance

  • All secondary classes
  • Every seat a deliberate decision
  • Reviewed and revised regularly
  • Support needs near teacher path
  • Anxiety-aware positioning

⚖ Ethics

Students should not be told the reasoning behind placements in ways that stigmatise them. Adjustments must be made discreetly. Student dignity protected at every stage.

References

Wannarka, R., & Ruhl, K. (2008). Support for Learning, 23(2), 89–93.

Marzano, R. J., et al. (2003). Classroom Management That Works. ASCD.

An end-of-unit topic test for Stage 5 Mathematics, combined with a Microsoft OneNote workflow for digital submission and inline written feedback — returned to students before the next lesson.

Learning Goal

My goal is an assessment practice where marking leads directly to a change in teaching — and where feedback reaches students quickly enough to genuinely influence their next attempt.

Why it addresses Standard 5

APST Standard 5 requires teachers to assess, provide feedback, and use evidence to modify teaching. Analysing error patterns across the class reveals which concepts need reteaching — this is Standard 5.4 in direct action. The OneNote workflow enables the three qualities Hattie & Timperley (2007) identify as making feedback effective: timely, targeted, and actionable. Black & Wiliam (1998) found embedding formative assessment within the teaching cycle produces significantly greater learning gains than summative-only approaches.

NESA Alignment

MA5.1-1WM, MA5.2-1WM — Working mathematically, Stage 5. Applicable across all content strands.

Learning Reflection

"Assessment is not something that happens after teaching — it is something that shapes what teaching happens next."

At a Glance

  • Stage 5, end-of-unit test
  • Whole-class error analysis
  • Targeted review from data
  • OneNote — digital submission
  • Inline feedback before next lesson

⚖ Ethics

All results handled confidentially. OneNote feedback visible only to individual student and teacher. Feedback framed constructively — never as comparison between students.

References

Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (1998). Assessment in Education, 5(1), 7–74.

Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). Review of Educational Research, 77(1), 81–112.

Conclusion

Reflecting on the Journey

What I have learned, where I am going, and what this portfolio represents.

Professional Learning Goals — Entering the Classroom

🎯
Design for Learners
Build resources that meet students where they are, not where the syllabus assumes
🔄
Reflect & Revise
Treat every lesson as data — use what happens to improve what happens next
📈
Keep Growing
These resources are a starting point — every classroom will teach me how to improve them

This portfolio is not a record of what I have done — it is a statement of who I am becoming as a teacher. The five resources represent my current best thinking about effective secondary Mathematics teaching, grounded in research, aligned to the APST Standards, and designed with real students in mind.

Each resource reflects a learning goal: design for diverse learners (Standard 1), teach with multiple representations (Anthony & Walshaw, 2009) (Standard 2), plan sequences that build genuine understanding (Kilpatrick et al., 2001) (Standard 3), create safe classrooms where students participate (Marzano et al., 2003) (Standard 4), and use assessment to improve teaching, not just measure it (Black & Wiliam, 1998) (Standard 5).

These are goals, not achievements. As Darling-Hammond & Bransford (2005) argue, becoming a teacher is a career-long process. This portfolio is the beginning.

Final Reflection

"The resources in this portfolio were designed for students I have not yet met in a classroom of my own. I look forward to the moment each resource meets reality and teaches me something new about how to improve it."

Learning Goals

  • Design for diverse learners
  • Teach with multiple representations
  • Plan sequences, not just tasks
  • Build proactive, safe classrooms
  • Let assessment drive teaching

Areas to Develop

  • Stronger extension tasks
  • Faster feedback turnaround
  • More student-led discussion
  • Broader assessment repertoire

Looking Forward

I am preparing for a career in secondary Mathematics. Every resource here will be tested, refined, and improved the moment it meets a real classroom — and that is exactly the point.
